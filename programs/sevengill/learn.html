Plate I · Field Notes

The Broadnose Sevengill, in brief.

A short, deliberately non-academic introduction. For citations, methods, and the technical literature, see our research notes.

§ I Identification

A counting trick.

Almost every shark you'll see has five gill slits. The broadnose sevengill has seven, and they're easy to count from a side-on photograph. The body is heavyset and tapered, the snout is short and rounded, and a single dorsal fin sits well back along the body, closer to the tail than to the head.

Adults commonly reach about 3 metres total length, with females growing larger than males. Juveniles are sub-metre and frequently mistaken for other species. The freckle pattern, which is what we identify individuals by, is denser on the head and pectoral region and fades toward the tail. How stable that pattern is over the animal's lifetime is one of the open scientific questions this catalogue is built to address.

§ II Where They Live

Cool, coastal, and circumglobal.

Sevengills favour cool temperate waters, usually 7–22 °C, and are found in shallow bays as well as on the continental shelf out to about 130 m. The species ranges across the Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian Oceans in two disjunct populations: a northern band along the western Americas and a southern band that wraps from South Africa through southern Australia to the southern cone of South America.

In our home range (the San Francisco Bay and the outer California coast), they aggregate in late spring and summer in shallow embayments and move offshore in winter. Studies of San Francisco Bay populations have documented site fidelity across multiple seasons; building a longer photographic record of who returns and who does not is one of this catalogue's primary goals.

§ III Why They Matter

Apex, slow, and under-studied.

As a coastal apex predator, the sevengill regulates populations of smaller sharks, rays, and bony fish in the bays where it hunts. It is aplacental viviparous (yolk-sac live-bearing): females carry a single large litter (commonly reported in the range of 60 to 100 pups, occasionally more) for an estimated gestation period of around 12 months. Females reach sexual maturity slowly, with age estimates in the published literature ranging widely but generally placing maturity in the second or third decade of life. That long generation time, combined with selective fishing pressure on adults, makes the species slow to recover from population declines.

The IUCN currently lists the species as Vulnerable, with population trends decreasing. Robust photographic data on individual movement, residency, and growth is the main thing missing, and it's the main thing this catalogue is trying to provide.

§ IV For Anglers

If you catch one, release it, but photograph it first.

Most sevengill encounters in our home waters come from rod-and-reel. Anglers see more of these animals than divers do; their photographs are often the best images we get. If you're targeting bay sharks or just hooked one by accident, here's how to make the catch count twice:

Keep it in the water if you can. A boat-side photo with the head clear of the surface is usually enough, no need to lift the animal aboard.

One clean side-profile of the head, before the release. Eye to last gill slit, sharp focus, sun behind you. That single frame is worth a season of fieldwork.

Note the location and time. If your phone is in airplane mode, it won't tag GPS. Either re-enable location services before snapping, or jot down the spot in the notes field of the submission form.

Release as quickly as possible. Sevengills are slow-growing, late-maturing, and listed as Vulnerable. Quick photograph, careful release, hooks cut if needed.

§ V For Divers

If you encounter one underwater.

Aim for the head. The freckle pattern from the eye to the last (seventh) gill slit is the most identifiable region. A clean profile from either side is gold; both sides is better.

Hold still, get close, no flash. Sevengills tolerate slow approaches well. Zoom and burst-mode are your friends; flash is not.

Don't crop or filter. Submit the original photo file at full resolution, with EXIF intact. Our model reads metadata for time and place. Cropped versions are still useful, but the originals do more.

Respect the animal. No baiting, no chasing, no touching. They're large, predatory, and protected. Photograph the ones that approach you, not the other way around.
